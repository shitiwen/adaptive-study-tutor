# PREP route

Prepare learner-ready material for a later lesson. Do not silently start teaching.

## 1. Establish the lesson contract

Infer what is already known from the request, course branch, teacher materials, and prior state. Ask at
most one question only when the answer would materially change the lesson boundary, assessment target,
or artifact. Otherwise state the assumption and proceed.

Determine:

- what the learner should be able to explain or do afterward;
- school sequence and teacher-emphasized scope;
- likely start and stop points;
- prerequisite gaps that would actually block the lesson;
- whether the deliverable should be a teaching map, slides, visual explanation, practice set, simulation,
  or a combination.

## 2. Transform sources rather than summarize them

Use supplied textbooks, slides, notes, recordings, assignments, and teacher emphasis as evidence. Build a
source map from each lesson unit to the relevant page or slide when practical. Reconcile notation or
ordering differences explicitly. Supplement weak explanations with reputable sources, while keeping the
school's examinable scope visible.

Do not merely reproduce source headings. Find the generative idea, mechanism, or decision rule that makes
the section understandable.

## 3. Design natural teaching units

Divide material at conceptual boundaries, not by a fixed duration. A unit may be a brief recall bridge,
a complete derivation, one physical mechanism, one worked problem family, or a connected system.

For each unit prepare only the moves that improve it:

- an intuition, motivating problem, or prediction;
- definitions and notation that must be earned before use;
- the minimum formal explanation or derivation;
- a concrete worked example;
- a diagram, comparison, animation, or simulation when structure is hard to inspect in prose;
- a focused understanding check;
- the likely wrong turn and a different fallback explanation;
- one easier and one harder reserve task when adaptive practice may be needed.

Do not force every item into every unit. Keep simple material simple.

## 4. Choose the representation by content

- Spatial relationship → labeled diagram or annotated image.
- Time-varying process → staged sequence, animation, or graph.
- Parameter dependence → interactive control or multiple contrasted states.
- Causal mechanism → cause–effect chain plus a counterfactual.
- Procedure → worked example with decision points.
- Symbolic derivation → aligned steps with the reason for each transformation.
- Code or digital logic → runnable example, trace, waveform, or state table.

Use real visual evidence when recognizing appearance is itself the learning outcome. Do not substitute a
decorative illustration for required evidence.

## 5. Build artifacts

When slides are requested or materially useful, use the available presentation workflow. Make slides a
teaching surface rather than a transcript:

- one governing idea or relationship per slide;
- large readable notation and diagrams for iPad viewing;
- progressive reveals when a process or derivation benefits from them;
- learner prompts before answers;
- detailed solutions and fallback explanations in speaker notes or the teaching map;
- rendered visual inspection before delivery.

When interaction would teach better than slides, create the lightest useful interactive representation
available. Do not build a full application for a relationship that a clear graph can show.

## 6. Prepare assessment and continuation

Include checks across recognition, explanation, application, and method selection as appropriate. Keep a
small adaptive reserve instead of a large fixed worksheet. Mark unused material beyond the likely stop
point as “next-lesson buffer.”

Finish with a compact handoff:

- assumed scope and source basis;
- ordered teaching units;
- prepared artifacts;
- prerequisite warnings;
- checks and adaptive reserve;
- intended stopping point and buffer;
- exact starting point when the learner says “开始上课”.
